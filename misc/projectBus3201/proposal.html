Purchase Intention of University Students in Dhaka: Digital Platforms versus Offline Shops

Digital platformsOffline shops

Research Proposal (common topic) | BUS3201 Research Methodology | Fall 2026

1. Background

Students in Dhaka can buy things in two ways. They can buy on digital platforms, such as Daraz, Facebook shops, brand websites and shopping apps. They can also buy in offline shops, such as shops, shopping malls and local markets that they visit in person.

Purchase intention means how willing a person is to buy, or how much they plan to buy, through a channel. A channel is the way a person buys: here, digital platforms or offline shops.

University students use smartphones and social media every day. They are also careful with money. So they may like digital platforms for low prices and saving time, but still like offline shops because they can see and touch the product and take it home at once.

2. Research problem

Many studies in Bangladesh look only at online shopping. They treat offline shops as a background choice. In Dhaka, offline shops are still busy and easy to reach. It is not clearly known whether students plan to buy more on digital platforms or in offline shops, or what makes them want to buy on digital platforms. It is also not known whether these intentions differ between groups of students, such as public and private university students, or male and female students, or whether students’ money and feelings of safety are related to their online buying. This study would look at both channels together.

3. Research objectives

  1. To compare digital purchase intention between public and private university students.
  2. To compare the proportion of male and female students who bought something online in the past 30 days.
  3. To compare students’ purchase intention for digital platforms and for offline shops.
  4. To examine the effect of price value, convenience and trust on digital purchase intention.
  5. To examine the relation between feeling safe when buying from digital platforms and the plan to buy on digital platforms.
  6. To examine the relation between monthly pocket money and online spending.

4. Research questions

  • RQ1. Is digital purchase intention different between public and private university students?
  • RQ2. Is the proportion of students who bought online in the past 30 days different between male and female students?
  • RQ3. Is students’ purchase intention for digital platforms different from their purchase intention for offline shops?
  • RQ4. Do price value, convenience and trust affect students’ digital purchase intention?
  • RQ5. Is there a relation between how safe students feel when buying from digital platforms and how much they plan to buy on digital platforms?
  • RQ6. Is there a relation between students’ monthly pocket money and the money they spend on digital platforms?

5. Significance of the study

This study is useful for students, sellers and researchers. For students, it shows which factors shape their shopping decisions and why they may prefer one channel. For online sellers, it shows the importance of fair prices, convenience and trust. For offline shops, it shows why physical shops still matter: buyers can check the product, take it home at once and feel more confident.

6. Literature review

6.1 Purchase intention

Purchase intention is the closest sign that a person will buy (Blackwell et al., 2006). Shoppers do not use only one channel. They move between online and offline channels because each channel gives a different benefit (Beck & Rygl, 2015; Dholakia et al., 2010; Neslin, 2022). In Dhaka, online shopping has not replaced physical shopping; the two channels exist together (Hossain et al., 2024).

6.2 Price value

Price value means a buyer feels that what they get is worth the money they pay (Zeithaml, 1988; Sweeney & Soutar, 2001). On digital platforms, discounts and easy price comparison can raise price value, and higher value can raise purchase intention (Chen & Dubinsky, 2003; Gan & Wang, 2017).

6.3 Convenience

Convenience means a channel saves time and effort and makes buying easy (Jiang et al., 2013). Convenience raises the intention to shop online (Chiang & Dholakia, 2003). Studies of university students in Bangladesh found the same result (Al Asheq et al., 2022; Aziz & Wahid, 2018).

6.4 Trust

Trust means a buyer believes the seller will deliver the right product, keep payment safe and handle problems fairly (Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; McKnight et al., 2002). Online buyers cannot touch the product before paying, so trust matters more online. In Bangladesh, trust is an important driver of online purchase intention (Khan & Ahmed, 2012; Rahman et al., 2018; Rana, 2022). So a student who feels safe buying online may also plan to buy more online.

6.5 Differences between student groups

Students do not all have the same money or the same shopping habits. In Bangladesh, private university fees are much higher than public university fees, so many private university students come from families with higher income. More money can mean more online buying, so digital purchase intention may differ between public and private university students. In the same way, students with more pocket money may spend more on digital platforms. Male and female students may also have different shopping habits, so this study checks whether the share of students who bought online in the past 30 days is different for men and women.

6.6 Research gap

Most local studies look only at online shopping. Few studies compare purchase intention for digital platforms and offline shops among the same students, and also compare groups of students (public and private universities, male and female). This study fills that gap for university students in Dhaka.

7. Variables and measurement

A Likert scale is a rating scale where the respondent shows how much they agree with a statement. This study would use 5 points: 1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree. Each main variable has 4 statements. The score of a variable is the average (mean) of its 4 statements, so the score is a number from 1 to 5.

Treatment of Likert data: the average score of 4 statements would be considered as numerical data (numbers), and the assumptions needed to use this data for the z-test, t-test and regression would be made. A single Likert statement would be treated as ordinal data: its answers have a clear order, but the gaps between answers are not fixed amounts.

8. Hypotheses and the test for each

The 5% level of significance (α = 0.05) would be used. All tests would be two-tailed: the alternative hypothesis (H₁) says “different” (≠), not “higher” or “lower”. Decision rule: if p < α, H₀ would be rejected (the data support H₁). If p ≥ α, the decision would be to fail to reject H₀.

No. Null hypothesis (H₀) Alternative hypothesis (H₁) Test
H1 Mean digital purchase intention of public university students = mean of private university students. (μ_public = μ_private) Mean digital purchase intention of public university students ≠ mean of private university students. (μ_public ≠ μ_private) z-test for two means (each group has at least 30 students)
H2 Proportion of male students who bought online in the past 30 days = proportion of female students. (p_male = p_female) Proportion of male students who bought online in the past 30 days ≠ proportion of female students. (p_male ≠ p_female) z-test for two proportions
H3 Students’ mean digital purchase intention = their mean offline purchase intention. (mean difference = 0) Students’ mean digital purchase intention ≠ their mean offline purchase intention. (mean difference ≠ 0) Paired t-test (the same students answer both)
H4 Price value, convenience and trust together have no effect on digital purchase intention. (β₁ = β₂ = β₃ = 0) At least one of price value, convenience and trust has an effect on digital purchase intention. (at least one β ≠ 0) Multiple linear regression (overall F-test)
H4a Price value has no effect on digital purchase intention. (β₁ = 0) Price value has an effect on digital purchase intention. (β₁ ≠ 0) Multiple regression: p-value of PV
H4b Convenience has no effect on digital purchase intention. (β₂ = 0) Convenience has an effect on digital purchase intention. (β₂ ≠ 0) Multiple regression: p-value of CV
H4c Trust has no effect on digital purchase intention. (β₃ = 0) Trust has an effect on digital purchase intention. (β₃ ≠ 0) Multiple regression: p-value of TR
H5 There is no relation between feeling safe buying on digital platforms (Q29) and the plan to buy on digital platforms (Q10). (ρs = 0) There is a relation between them. (ρs ≠ 0) Spearman rank correlation
H6 There is no relation between monthly pocket money and online spending in the past 30 days. (ρ = 0) There is a relation between them. (ρ ≠ 0) Pearson correlation

Regression model: DPI = b₀ + b₁·PV + b₂·CV + b₃·TR

Why Spearman for H5: Q29 and Q10 are single Likert statements, so they are ordinal (ordered) data. The order is clear (Agree is higher than Neutral), but the gap between two answers is not a fixed amount. So the scores themselves would not be used; their ranks would be used, and the Spearman test compares the ranks.

Why Pearson for H6: monthly pocket money and Spend30 are ratio data: true numbers with a real zero and equal gaps (1,000 Taka is always 1,000 Taka). So the actual values can be used, and the Pearson test fits.

9. Methodology

9.1 Research design

This study would be quantitative, because numbers would be collected and tested with statistics. It would be cross-sectional, because data would be collected only once. It would be descriptive and comparative, because students would be described and groups and channels would be compared.

9.2 Population and sampling

Population: university students in Dhaka.

Sampling method: quota sampling. Quota sampling is a non-probability method where the researcher decides how many people to take from each group, then fills each group with people who are easy to reach. Equal numbers of male and female students, and equal numbers of public and private university students, would be taken. Respondents who choose “Prefer not to say” for gender would be thanked but not counted in the gender quota.

Sample size: the minimum would be 15 students in each of the 4 cells defined by gender and type of university, so the minimum sample would be 15 × 4 = 60. Each cell can have between 15 and 20 students, so the total sample would be between 60 and 80 students. With at least 15 per cell, each group in H1 and H2 (public, private, male, female) would have at least 30 students, which the z-test needs.

9.3 Data collection

  • Tool: a Google Form questionnaire would be used (see the questionnaire).
  • Pilot test: before the main survey, 10 to 15 students would fill the form. Every question would be checked for clarity, and unclear questions would be fixed.
  • Ethics: the first question would ask for consent (permission). Names, phone numbers and emails would not be asked. Answers would be anonymous, which means no one can tell who gave which answer.
  • Data for analysis: the main analysis would use a simulated practice dataset. A computer made this data for learning; it is not from real people. The course teacher gives one dataset to each student (one per Student ID) on the data page. The pilot responses from real students would be reported separately.

9.4 Limitations

  1. Non-random sample: quota sampling does not choose people at random, so the results may not represent all university students in Dhaka.
  2. Test assumptions: the z-test, t-test and correlation tests assume a random sample. Because the sample would not be random, the p-values should be read with care.
  3. Recall error: online spending (Spend30) and pocket money would be answered from memory, so some amounts may not be exact.
  4. No funding: the study has no budget, so respondents cannot be paid and students across all universities in Dhaka cannot be reached. This would limit the sample to students who are easy to reach.
  5. Limited time: the study would be done within one semester, while the researchers take two or three other courses at the same time. This would limit the sample size and the time for data collection.
