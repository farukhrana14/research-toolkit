1 · Population and sample

Population ≠ "People or human necessarily"

PopulationEvery item we want to know about: all the people, packets, bolts, transactions or T-shirts that fit our question.
SampleThe part of the population we actually pick and measure.

A population does not have to be all the humans of a country or a city, and it does not necessarily have to be human: it may be human or may not be. It may be milk packs, bolts, invoices or T-shirts, whatever we want to measure and study.

1 · Population and sample

Examples

PopulationSample
Only about 50 people in the world are known to have the Rh-null blood group.The population is so small that we can study all of them. This is a census: when the population is tiny, sampling isn't needed.
Aarong Dairy produces about 5 million packs of 500 ml pasteurised milk a year in Bangladesh (illustrative).Pick 60 packs of Aarong 500 ml milk from 60 different shops.
A nut-bolt factory makes 10 million bolts a year.Pick 200 bolts at random across all shifts and measure each one's diameter.
Bangladesh produces about 2–3 billion T-shirts a year for export (approx.).Each buying house inspects a sample of every order, as per AQL, which is about 3%, to check it meets their specs.

We use the sample's numbers (x̄, s) to learn about the population's numbers (μ, σ).

2 · Sampling distribution

One sample is not the whole story

If we take a new sample, we get a slightly different mean. Take many samples, write down each mean, and those means form their own distribution: the sampling distribution of the mean.

Example 1 · Aarong milkEvery week, QC picks 60 packs and records the average volume. Week 1: 499.6 ml, week 2: 500.3 ml, week 3: 500.1 ml … After a year we have 52 averages, and they cluster tightly around 500 ml.
Example 2 · BoltsEach shift, 30 bolts are measured and the average diameter is recorded: 10.02 mm, 9.98 mm, 10.01 mm … The shift averages bunch up around 10 mm, much closer together than single bolts do.

2 · From sampling distribution to normal

Central Limit Theorem

If the population is normal, or the sample size n ≥ 30,
then the sample means follow a normal distribution.
CentreThe averages centre on the population mean μ.
Spread: averages are steadier than single itemsOne bolt can be far from 10 mm, but in an average of 25 bolts the big ones and small ones cancel out.
Spread of averages = σ ÷ √n
Single bolts: σ = 0.1 mm → averages of 25 bolts: 0.1 ÷ √25 = 0.1 ÷ 5 = 0.02 mm
ShapeBell-shaped, even when single items are skewed.

This is why the normal distribution sits at the heart of statistics.

3 · The normal distribution

The bell curve

A normal distribution is fixed by just two numbers:

μ = centre
σ = spread

3 · The normal distribution

Three properties

1
Symmetric bell shapeThe left half mirrors the right half. Mean = median = mode, all at the peak.
2
Tails never touch the axisThe curve gets closer and closer to zero on both sides but never reaches it.
3
Total area = 1 (100%)Area under the curve is probability. About 68% lies within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ and 99.7% within ±3σ.

4 · The Z-score

Z = how many σ from the mean

Z = (X − μ) ÷ σ
Example · Aarong milkμ = 500 ml, σ = 4 ml. One pack holds 508 ml.
Z = (508 − 500) ÷ 4 = +2.00, so it's 2σ above the mean.
Example · Aarong milkAnother pack holds 495 ml.
Z = (495 − 500) ÷ 4 = −1.25, so it's 1.25σ below the mean.

5 · Is my data normal?

Why we must check first

The Z-table's areas (34.1%, 13.6%, 2.1% …) are only correct when the data is bell-shaped. If the data is skewed, the table gives wrong probabilities.

Check the shape→Roughly normal?→Use Z and the Z-table

5 · Is my data normal?

Skewness and kurtosis

MeasureWhat it checksIdealTolerable
SkewnessIs one tail longer than the other?0between ±1 and ±2
KurtosisAre the tails too heavy or too light (peak too sharp or too flat)?0between ±1 and ±2

Kurtosis here means excess kurtosis, which is 0 for a normal curve. Some books report raw kurtosis, where normal = 3.

Formal tests also exist, such as Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov. For classroom practice we use skewness and kurtosis.

6 · What normality lets us do

What can we say once data is normal?

1
About single itemsIf the pack volumes are normal, we can say: "About 10.6% of Aarong packs will hold less than 495 ml." This is true for future packs too, as long as the factory keeps working the same way.
2
About the average of all packsAverages of samples are normal (when the data is normal, or the sample has 30 or more items). So from one sample we can say: "We are 95% sure the true average of all packs is between 498.2 and 500.2 ml." This is called a confidence interval.

This is inference: a careful estimate with a stated level of confidence. It is not a forecast.

7 · Reading the Z-table

Row + column = your Z

Open the Z-Table ↗. It gives the area to the LEFT of Z. Split Z into a row part (first decimal) and a column part (second decimal).

Positive Z = 1.231.23 = 1.2 + 0.03
→ row 1.2, column .03
Negative Z = −1.25Go one row further down: −1.3 + 0.05 = −1.25
→ row −1.3, column .05

Once you have the row and the column, the area is the value where the row and column meet.

This method is for our Z-Table. Some printed tables arrange negative values differently.

8 · Worked example 1 of 4 · Less than (left side)

Aarong 500 ml packs: μ = 500 ml, σ = 4 ml, volumes are normal.

What percentage of packs hold less than 495 ml?

Solution

Z = (495 − 500) ÷ 4 = −1.25
Z-table: row −1.3, column .05 → 0.1056
P(X < 495) = 10.56%

The table already gives the left area: read it straight off. Z-Table ↗

8 · Worked example 2 of 4 · More than (right side)

Aarong 500 ml packs: μ = 500 ml, σ = 4 ml, volumes are normal.

What percentage of packs hold more than 508 ml?

Solution

Z = (508 − 500) ÷ 4 = 2.00
Z-table: row 2.0, column .00 → 0.9772 (left area)
P(X > 508) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228 = 2.28%

Right side = 1 − left side. Z-Table ↗

8 · Worked example 3 of 4 · Between two values

Aarong 500 ml packs: μ = 500 ml, σ = 4 ml, volumes are normal.

What percentage of packs hold between 494 and 506 ml?

Solution

Z₁ = (494 − 500) ÷ 4 = −1.50 → row −1.5, column .00 → 0.0668
Z₂ = (506 − 500) ÷ 4 = 1.50 → row 1.5, column .00 → 0.9332
P = 0.9332 − 0.0668 = 0.8664 = 86.64%

Larger left area − smaller left area. Z-Table ↗

8 · Worked example 4 of 4 · Finding X from a percentage

Aarong 500 ml packs: μ = 500 ml, σ = 4 ml, volumes are normal.

Below what volume are the lowest 10% of packs?

Solution

Find 0.1000 inside the table: closest is 0.1003 at row −1.3, column .02 → Z = −1.3 + 0.02 = −1.28
X = μ + Z · σ = 500 + (−1.28 × 4) = 494.88 ml

Work backwards: area → Z → X. Z-Table ↗

9 · Confidence interval

Estimating the population mean

CI = x̄ ± Z* · (σ ÷ √n)

Z* = 1.645 (90%) · 1.96 (95%) · 2.576 (99%)

Example

60 Aarong packs from 60 shops: x̄ = 499.2 ml, σ = 4 ml.
E = 1.96 × (4 ÷ √60) = 1.96 × 0.516 = 1.01
95% CI = 499.2 ± 1.01 = (498.19, 500.21) ml

Meaning: we are 95% confident the true average volume of all Aarong 500 ml packs is between 498.19 and 500.21 ml.

10 · Summary

The path we followed

Population → sample: we measure a part to learn about the whole. Sampling distribution: sample means form their own distribution. Central Limit Theorem: those means are normal when the population is normal or n ≥ 30. Normal curve: two parameters (μ, σ), three properties, 68–95–99.7. Check normality: skewness and kurtosis ideally 0, tolerable ±1 to ±2. Z-score and Z-table: probabilities for items, confidence intervals for the mean.
